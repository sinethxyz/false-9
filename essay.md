# False 9

People have called me a generalist. Sometimes a jack of all trades.

I understand why.

I move between product, engineering, design, strategy, branding, research and systems. I can spend an hour thinking about software architecture, move into a landing page, realise that the real problem is positioning, restructure the product around that insight, then return to the technology.

From the outside, that looks like breadth. Sometimes it probably looks like distraction.

But I think generalist and False 9 describe two different things.

A generalist is defined by range.

They can play many positions.

A False 9 is defined by movement through a system.

They understand how the positions relate to one another, where the system is currently constrained, and which position needs to be occupied now.

A generalist asks:

**What can I do?**

A False 9 asks:

**What does the field need?**

That distinction matters to me.

I do have broad interests and broad capabilities. But breadth is not the philosophy.

The philosophy is that I do not want any one capability to become my permanent position.

I want to read the field, understand its current state, enter wherever I can create the most leverage, change something, release the work, and move again.

The closest analogy I have found for this comes from football, or soccer if you are American.

It is called the **False 9**.

In football, the number 9 traditionally refers to the central striker. The player closest to the opponent’s goal. Think of him as the endpoint of the attack. His teammates progress the ball up the field, and he stays high, occupies defenders and tries to score.

His position creates an expectation.

If you are a defender, you generally know where the striker is supposed to be.

The False 9 deliberately breaks that expectation.

Instead of remaining close to goal, the striker starts dropping backwards into midfield.

Now the defender has a problem.

If he follows, he abandons the defensive line and leaves space behind him.

If he stays, the False 9 receives the ball freely between the lines and can turn towards goal.

Either decision changes the geometry of the game.

Lionel Messi became one of the defining examples of this role.

You do not need to know football to understand Messi’s significance. He is widely regarded as one of the greatest players the sport has produced, but what interests me here is not the trophies, goals or highlight reels.

It is the way he understood space.

Messi might begin an attack as the most advanced player on the field, then drop twenty metres towards midfield. He would receive the ball, combine with another player, pull defenders towards him, create space somewhere else, release the ball, then appear near the goal again seconds later.

On a team sheet, he had a position.

During the game, that position was almost useless for describing what he actually did.

His movement could look positionless.

It was not.

He was responding to the structure of the game.

That is the distinction that made the idea click for me.

It is also how I increasingly think about building companies, products and systems.

I do not move between disciplines simply because I enjoy doing different things.

I move because the location of the most important problem changes.

My position can change while the objective remains stable.

If the thing preventing a product from existing is technical, I move into engineering.

If the technology works but nobody understands why the product should exist, I move into positioning.

If people understand it but the experience is terrible, I move into design.

If the product is good but nobody knows it exists, I move towards distribution.

If all of those functions keep producing inconsistencies because the organisation itself has no structure, I stop solving surface problems and move into the system underneath them.

I am not asking:

**What is my role?**

I am asking:

**Where is the constraint?**

But even that is incomplete.

The False 9 should not merely move towards a constraint.

He should move towards the **highest leverage constraint available now**.

That word matters.

Now.

There is one rule above almost everything else in this philosophy.

**The False 9 has to stay present.**

The field only exists now.

Yesterday’s bottleneck may already be gone. Tomorrow’s problem may never arrive. A plan can be useful, but the moment I become more loyal to the plan than to the current state of reality, I stop reading the game.

Presence is what allows leverage to become visible.

I keep returning to three questions.

**What is true right now?**

**What matters most right now?**

**What single move would change the most right now?**

That is the filter.

Not the most interesting move.

Not the move that makes me look busiest.

Not the move that happens to use my favourite skill.

Not the move I decided three weeks ago that I wanted to make.

The highest leverage move.

Sometimes that is building.

Sometimes it is deleting.

Sometimes it is making a decision.

Sometimes it is talking to one person.

Sometimes it is documenting something.

Sometimes it is fixing the information system underneath everything else.

Sometimes it is refusing to act because the system has not produced enough information yet.

The False 9 should not optimise for motion.

He should optimise for leverage.

And leverage is contextual.

The same action can be brilliant in one state of the system and completely useless in another.

That is why presence matters.

You cannot find the highest leverage move if you are not actually looking at the current field.

So the basic operating loop becomes:

**Be present. Read the field. Find the constraint. Choose the highest leverage move. Act. Observe again.**

Everything else sits underneath that.

And reading the field requires intuition.

A False 9 needs intuition.

Not intuition in the mystical sense.

I mean the ability to absorb context, recognise patterns, feel where the structure is changing and form a useful judgment before every variable has been made explicit.

Without intuition, positional freedom becomes random movement.

The False 9 works because he is reading things that are difficult to reduce to a checklist. Pressure. Timing. Body orientation. Momentum. Space that is about to open rather than space that is already obvious.

I think building works the same way.

Sometimes I can look at a product and feel that the interface is not actually the problem. Something deeper is wrong with the model underneath it.

Sometimes a company appears to have a distribution problem, but the product itself has not yet become legible enough to distribute.

Sometimes everybody is trying to solve the visible bottleneck while the real constraint sits one layer below it.

You can eventually analyse these things explicitly, and you should.

But the first signal is often intuitive.

That is why I do not think a False 9 is merely someone with broad skills.

Range gives you possible moves.

Context tells you which moves are relevant.

Intuition helps you see the move before it becomes obvious.

But intuition alone is not enough either.

A False 9 also needs to grasp concepts quickly.

If you are going to move between positions, you cannot require months of immersion every time you enter a new layer of the system.

You need to be able to arrive somewhere unfamiliar, identify the important concepts, understand how they relate, and construct a useful mental model quickly.

That does not mean pretending to be an expert.

A specialist may understand a domain far more deeply than I do.

The advantage I care about is different.

**Time to useful understanding.**

When I enter a new area, I am trying to find the primitives first.

What are the few concepts everything else depends on?

What are the constraints?

What causes what?

What depends on what?

Which details matter to the decision in front of me?

Which details can remain unloaded?

Where does this system resemble something I already understand?

I absorb.

I map.

I connect.

I compress.

Then I try to use the model.

That ability matters because a False 9 cannot afford to treat every new position as an entirely new universe.

The faster I can reduce an unfamiliar domain to its underlying structure, the faster I can interact with specialists, ask better questions, identify the real constraint and make a useful move.

Again, the goal is not to know everything.

It is to know enough of the right things quickly enough to change the state of the game.

There is a cognitive component to this that I find difficult to separate from the philosophy.

You need context.

Almost like RAM.

The more of the relevant field you can hold in active context at once, the easier it becomes to see relationships between things that appear separate when viewed from individual positions.

Engineering sees an engineering problem.

Design sees a design problem.

Distribution sees a distribution problem.

Finance sees a finance problem.

Operations sees an operations problem.

I want to temporarily load enough of the whole system into context to ask a different question:

**What is actually happening here?**

But holding context is not enough.

The important part is synthesis.

I want to connect the pieces, find the relationships between them, and then compress them.

Twenty observations become five relationships.

Five relationships become one model.

One model reveals a constraint.

The constraint suggests a move.

That is the paradox of how I like to operate.

I want enormous complexity internally and extreme simplicity externally.

**Context. Synthesis. Compression. Action.**

And it has to happen quickly.

The field is moving while you are reading it.

A perfect analysis of a state that no longer exists is not particularly useful.

Context has a half life.

The correct move at one moment may no longer be the correct move ten minutes, ten days or ten months later.

That is why speed matters.

Not speed as frantic motion.

Speed as the ability to synthesise enough of the field while your understanding of it is still relevant.

This is another reason I keep coming back to Messi.

For someone capable of almost absurd technical complexity, he often made the game look simple.

One touch.

A short pass.

A change of direction.

Carry the ball a few metres.

Release it.

Move again.

That simplicity is deceptive.

Before making a five metre pass, a player at that level is processing the positions of teammates, defenders, velocity, pressure, available space, body orientation, momentum and what the field might look like several seconds later.

An enormous amount of information gets compressed into one small action.

I think building works the same way.

My brain naturally wants to see the entire system.

If I am thinking about a company, I can very quickly find myself thinking about the product, technology, business model, infrastructure, branding, distribution, future products, partnerships, organisation and what the company could become years later.

That ability is useful.

But it becomes destructive when understanding the whole field creates an urge to play the whole game simultaneously.

I have had to learn that seeing twenty moves does not mean I should make twenty moves.

The goal is almost the opposite.

I want **complexity in perception and simplicity in action**.

Understand as much of the system as possible, then compress that understanding into the smallest effective intervention.

What is true right now?

What actually matters?

What is blocking movement?

What action changes the most?

Do that.

Then look again.

Simplicity, in that sense, is not the absence of deep thinking.

It is often evidence that the thinking worked.

A good engineer can study an enormous system and realise the best solution is deleting something.

A good designer can understand an entire workflow and remove three screens.

A good strategist can study fifty opportunities and conclude that the company should pursue one.

The visible output becomes smaller as the underlying understanding becomes better.

That is the kind of simplicity I want.

Not simplistic thinking.

Compressed thinking.

This is also why I increasingly prefer loops to rigid plans.

Every useful action changes the state of the system.

Suppose a company is currently in state A. I identify what appears to be the highest leverage constraint and make a move. The company is now in state B.

But state B contains information that did not exist in state A.

Maybe the prototype proves the engineering problem was easier than expected but reveals that users do not understand the product.

Maybe talking to customers destroys an assumption the roadmap was built around.

Maybe hiring someone removes one bottleneck and reveals another.

Maybe introducing an agent means work that required human attention yesterday no longer requires it today.

The next move should therefore be selected from the new state of the system.

That sounds obvious, but organisations constantly violate it.

They create long plans and then become emotionally attached to executing them even after reality has changed.

A roadmap tries to tell you the next twenty moves.

A good loop tells you **how to decide the next move twenty times**.

I trust the loop more.

Observe.

Understand.

Act.

Update.

The sophistication is not in making the loop complicated.

It is in becoming better at seeing.

The same principle applies to frameworks.

A False 9 should not be trapped by frameworks either.

Frameworks are useful because they compress experience. They give you a way to think faster. They help you recognise recurring structures.

But frameworks are maps.

They are not the field.

If I enter a situation and an existing framework fits, I will use it.

If it only partially fits, I will modify it.

If it distorts what is actually happening, I will throw it away and build another one.

The goal is not to become loyal to a model.

The goal is to understand reality.

That means a False 9 has to be capable of creating his own frameworks.

Not for the sake of sounding original.

Because new situations often require new abstractions.

I might take something from systems theory, computing, product design, psychology, organisational theory or football and combine it into a model that helps me understand the problem in front of me.

Then the model has to survive contact with reality.

If it works, I keep it.

If reality exposes a flaw, I change it.

If the situation changes, I adapt it.

The framework serves the field.

The field does not serve the framework.

This matters because one of the easiest ways to stop being present is to become too attached to a model.

You stop observing reality and start searching for evidence that reality resembles your framework.

I want the opposite.

Observe reality first.

Build the model from what is actually there.

Use the model.

Test it.

Adapt it.

The point of a framework is to increase clarity and speed, not to replace perception.

So the False 9 needs strong principles without rigid thinking.

Strong principles.

Flexible frameworks.

Dynamic execution.

There is another part of the False 9 that matters just as much.

The False 9 does not move only for himself.

His movement changes what becomes possible for everybody around him.

When he drops away from the defensive line, he may pull a defender with him. The space he leaves can then be attacked by a winger or midfielder.

He might touch the ball only once, but the important thing he created was not necessarily the pass.

It was the space.

I think building organisations works similarly.

The highest leverage thing I can do is not necessarily execute more work personally.

Sometimes it is to change the structure so that ten other actions become easier.

If I solve the same problem once, I have completed a task.

If I recognise why that problem keeps appearing and create a system that prevents it from appearing again, I have changed the field.

This creates another loop in the way I work.

**Do. Understand. Systemise. Delegate. Move.**

I still believe in doing the work first.

You cannot meaningfully abstract something you do not understand.

You cannot delegate a problem whose shape you have never seen.

You cannot design a good system entirely from theory.

So I enter the position.

I touch the work.

I understand the constraint.

But I do not want to remain there permanently.

If a problem repeats, I look for the pattern.

If the pattern can become a process, I create the process.

If the process can become software, infrastructure, documentation, a protocol or an agent, I move it there.

If somebody else can now own it better than I can, I transfer it.

Then I leave.

My goal is not to accumulate more and more responsibilities as the company grows.

It is almost the reverse.

**My next job should often be to make my previous job unnecessary.**

If I repeatedly organise information manually, build the information system.

If I repeatedly explain the same idea, document it.

If I repeatedly make the same decision, encode the principle.

If I repeatedly coordinate the same workflow, automate the workflow.

If I repeatedly correct the same agent mistake, change the context or interface the agent operates from.

The output I care about is not how indispensable I become.

It is how capable the system becomes without requiring my continuous presence.

This is where the football metaphor becomes slightly counterintuitive.

A False 9 looks free because he moves everywhere.

But that freedom only works because there is structure underneath him.

If everyone on the football pitch decided to become positionless at the same time, the system would collapse.

Messi could move because the players around him understood their own roles, the team’s principles and what his movement meant for them.

Freedom at one layer depended on organisation at another.

I think exactly the same thing is true for the kind of companies I want to build.

The more fluid I become, the more organised the foundation has to become.

If I am moving between engineering, product, strategy and design while humans and agents continue executing behind me, the organisation cannot depend on everybody vaguely remembering what I meant.

It needs a substrate of truth.

That means canonical sources.

Clear repositories.

Explicit ownership.

Decision records.

Versioned documentation.

Tests.

Interfaces.

Schemas.

Logs.

Known assumptions.

Known unknowns.

Clear separation between something that has been proposed and something that has actually been decided.

Because autonomous systems introduce a particularly dangerous failure mode.

**Organisational hallucination.**

An agent can confidently act on context that was never true.

One system can interpret a proposal as a decision.

Two agents can independently solve the same problem in incompatible ways.

Old documentation can remain technically valid while no longer representing the current direction.

A model can fill missing context with something plausible rather than something real.

If the human operating above all of this is also constantly moving between layers, those errors can compound incredibly quickly.

So the False 9 model requires something that sounds contradictory.

**Positionless at the top requires rigidity in the truth layer below.**

Or another way I think about it:

**Autonomy requires a harder substrate of truth.**

Every important move should ideally make the organisation more legible.

Observe.

Verify.

Act.

Record.

Check.

When I leave engineering and move into product, engineering should not have to guess what I meant.

When I return three days later, I should not have to reconstruct reality by scrolling through old conversations.

The system should be capable of telling me what was decided, why it was decided, what changed, what remains uncertain, who owns the next action and what the canonical state is now.

The organisation remembers so that I do not have to.

That matters because human working memory is terrible infrastructure.

If my brain has to continuously preserve every project’s state, every unresolved decision, every reason something changed, every agent responsibility and every dependency, then my brain stops being a thinking system and becomes storage.

I would rather externalise the storage.

Repositories, documentation, logs, maps, protocols and systems preserve state.

Then I can leave.

When I return, I reconstruct the field from the system itself.

In that sense, external structure creates internal freedom.

The system remembers the game.

I can go back to reading it.

There is another idea from computing that I find useful here: the separation between a **control plane** and an **execution plane**.

Very roughly, the execution plane performs the work.

The control plane determines how the system should behave.

I suspect autonomous companies will increasingly develop something similar.

Agents, software and specialised humans will perform more bounded execution.

They will write code, research questions, run tests, monitor systems, create assets, maintain documentation and operate workflows.

The human role does not disappear.

But the highest leverage human work may increasingly shift towards deciding what the system should be doing in the first place.

What should exist?

Which constraint matters?

Where should resources move?

Which assumptions are wrong?

What should remain human?

What should become automated?

Which interface is missing?

Which problem is actually three problems pretending to be one?

This is where the False 9 becomes more than a football metaphor for me.

The player moves between layers of the game.

Close enough to execution to understand reality.

Far enough out to see the structure.

Then back into execution when necessary.

I do not want to manage an organisation entirely from above.

The False 9 does not stand outside the pitch giving instructions.

He enters the game.

Touches the ball.

Feels the pressure.

Understands what is actually happening.

But he also refuses to become trapped by the position he entered.

That creates the larger loop I increasingly want my work to follow.

**Enter. Understand. Change. Encode. Transfer. Exit. Observe again.**

Every cycle should increase the capability of the organisation.

And every increase in capability reveals a new constraint.

There is no final bottleneck.

If you remove the thing limiting a system, something else becomes the limiting factor.

Solve engineering and distribution might become the bottleneck.

Solve distribution and onboarding might become the bottleneck.

Automate onboarding and infrastructure might become the bottleneck.

Expand infrastructure and organisational coordination might become the bottleneck.

A growing system is essentially a sequence of constraints revealing themselves.

So I do not need to know every problem the company will ever face.

I need a reliable way of finding the current one.

That is why being present is not some side note in the philosophy.

It is central.

If I am mentally living six months ahead, I can start solving constraints that do not exist yet.

If I am still emotionally attached to a problem from last week, I can keep optimising a part of the field that has already changed.

If I am trying to do ten things simultaneously, I stop seeing clearly enough to identify which one actually matters.

The False 9 has to keep returning to the present.

Not because the future does not matter.

Because the future is built through correctly reading the current state.

One move.

New state.

One move.

New state.

That is how the game progresses.

And this is where intuition and truth need each other.

Intuition helps me decide where to look.

It does not get to decide what is true.

That distinction is important.

A strong intuition can become dangerous if it never collides with reality.

You can become extremely confident in a model that only exists inside your own head.

So my preferred loop is not simply:

**Sense. Act.**

It is:

**Sense. Synthesise. Compress. Inspect. Verify. Act. Observe. Update.**

The intuition generates the hypothesis.

The system of truth tests it.

Reality updates the intuition.

Over time, good intuition becomes a kind of compressed pattern recognition.

You do not consciously calculate every variable from scratch every time because previous experience, feedback and models begin reducing the search space for you.

But the loop must remain open.

You can never become so attached to intuition that you stop letting reality correct you.

That would defeat the whole point.

This is why I resist reducing all of this to the word generalist.

A generalist is defined by range.

A False 9 is defined by movement through a system.

A generalist knows many positions.

A False 9 understands the relationship between the positions.

A generalist has more possible tools.

A False 9 needs enough context, synthesis and intuition to know which tool the field actually requires.

A generalist can play several roles.

A False 9 can enter a role, understand it quickly enough to become useful, change the structure around it, create space for somebody else to occupy it, and then leave.

Generalism is a property of the person.

False 9 is a property of how the person operates.

I may be a generalist by capability.

False 9 describes how I allocate those capabilities.

And there is an important warning inside this philosophy.

Positionless does not mean directionless.

Constantly moving because something new seems exciting is not False 9 behaviour.

That is just drift.

Jumping from one problem to another because they are stimulating is not leverage.

It is fragmentation.

The freedom to change positions only works when the objective is unusually clear.

The mission needs to be more stable than the role.

If I know what I am trying to build, I can change what I am doing today without losing direction.

Strong mission.

Flexible position.

Without the first, flexibility becomes chaos.

Without the second, structure becomes bureaucracy.

I want both.

And this is also why I think the False 9 may become increasingly relevant as organisations become more autonomous.

As agents become better at bounded execution, raw execution becomes more abundant.

Not irrelevant.

More abundant.

And whenever one resource becomes more abundant, another constraint becomes more important.

In this case, I think that constraint is increasingly judgment.

Knowing what should exist.

Knowing what matters now.

Knowing what not to build.

Seeing that the technical problem is actually a product problem.

Seeing that the product problem is actually an organisational problem.

Seeing that everyone is optimising their individual position while the field itself is broken.

Recognising the empty space before it becomes obvious.

The highest performing human in an autonomous organisation may not be the person who personally executes the most tasks.

It may be the person whose understanding of the field causes the most useful work to happen.

That is a different definition of leverage.

You do not optimise the player.

You optimise the field.

Sometimes the highest leverage move is writing the code yourself.

Sometimes it is designing the architecture so ten future pieces of code become trivial.

Sometimes it is making one product decision that deletes months of unnecessary work.

Sometimes it is defining a protocol so agents stop interpreting the same concept differently.

Sometimes it is noticing that a concept everybody is debating can be understood in twenty minutes once the correct abstraction is found.

Sometimes it is creating a framework that did not exist because none of the existing ones accurately describe the problem.

Sometimes it is adapting a framework that worked yesterday because the field has changed today.

Sometimes it is doing nothing for a moment because the current state has not produced enough information to justify another move.

The philosophy is not:

**Move constantly.**

It is:

**Move when the field tells you to.**

And move towards the thing that changes the most.

Maybe that is the deepest reason I like the Messi analogy.

He did not look rushed.

There was speed, but not panic.

There was complexity, but not clutter.

There was enormous processing, but often a simple output.

And most importantly, he seemed to understand something about the game before the game had fully revealed it to everyone else.

That is what I want from my own way of building.

To stay present enough to see the field as it actually is.

To hold enough context to understand it.

To grasp unfamiliar concepts quickly enough that a new domain does not become a wall.

To synthesise different layers into one coherent model.

To compress that model aggressively.

To create frameworks when existing ones are insufficient.

To adapt those frameworks when reality changes.

To never become more loyal to a framework than to the field itself.

To trust intuition enough to know where to look.

To distrust intuition enough to verify what I find.

To identify the highest leverage constraint.

To make one move at a time.

To make that move as effectively as possible.

To observe what changed.

To update the model.

To turn repeated knowledge into systems.

To turn systems into interfaces.

To turn interfaces into delegation.

To create positions that humans and machines can occupy reliably.

To keep the truth layer organised enough that none of us have to invent reality.

Then move again.

I am not trying to become the engineer.

Or the designer.

Or the strategist.

Or the marketer.

Or the operator.

I will enter any of those positions when the game requires it.

And I will leave them when it does not.

The goal is not to do everything.

The goal is not to know everything.

The goal is to understand enough of the field, quickly enough and clearly enough, to know what matters most now.

Then act on it.

**Be present.**

**Read the field.**

**Find the highest leverage constraint.**

**Grasp what you need to understand.**

**Build the model you need.**

**Synthesise.**

**Compress.**

**Make one move.**

**Make it count.**

**Release the ball.**

**Read the new field.**

**Move again.**

Maybe I am a generalist.

That describes the tools I carry.

It does not describe how I build.

**False 9 does.**
